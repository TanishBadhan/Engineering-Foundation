## 1. Frequency Distributions

A **frequency distribution** summarizes categorical data by showing how many observations belong to each category. It turns a list of responses into an organized summary, making it easier to compare categories and identify common responses.

### 1.1 Frequency and Relative Frequency

| Term | Meaning | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency (f) | Number of observations in a category. | Count |
| Relative frequency | Proportion of observations in a category. | f/n |
| Percentage | Relative frequency expressed as a percentage. | (f/n) × 100 |

Where n is the total number of observations.

**Worked example:** 20 students report their preferred beverage.

| Beverage | Frequency | Relative Frequency | Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Tea | 8 | 0.40 | 40% |
| Coffee | 6 | 0.30 | 30% |
| Juice | 4 | 0.20 | 20% |
| Water | 2 | 0.10 | 10% |
| **Total** | **20** | **1.00** | **100%** |

For Tea:

\[
\text{Relative frequency}=\frac{8}{20}=0.40
\]

\[
\text{Percentage}=0.40\times100=40\%
\]

The relationship can also be reversed:

\[
\text{Frequency}=n\times\text{Relative frequency}
\]

For example, 20 × 0.40 = 8 students prefer Tea.

### 1.2 Properties of a Frequency Distribution

- Total frequency equals the number of observations: \(\sum f=n\).
- Relative frequencies sum to 1.
- Percentages sum to 100%, before rounding.
- Categories should be clearly defined and non-overlapping.
- Relative frequencies make distributions from different sample sizes easier to compare because they show proportions rather than raw counts.

For **ordinal data**, cumulative frequency is the running total of frequencies in the categories' natural order. It is useful when locating the median category. Cumulative frequency is not meaningful for nominal categories because they have no natural order.

## 2. Graphical Representation

A frequency table gives exact counts. Graphs make patterns and comparisons easier to see, especially when a dataset contains many observations.

### 2.1 Bar Chart

A **bar chart** displays categories as separate rectangular bars. The height or length of each bar represents its frequency or relative frequency.

**Use it to:** Compare the number or proportion of observations across categories.

Example:

| Beverage | Frequency |
|---|---:|
| Tea | 8 |
| Coffee | 6 |
| Juice | 4 |
| Water | 2 |

```text
Tea     ████████  8
Coffee  ██████    6
Juice   ████      4
Water   ██        2
```

**Key features:**
- Bars have equal width and visible gaps because each bar represents a distinct category.
- The value axis shows frequency or percentage.
- Categories may be arranged alphabetically, in their natural order, or by frequency, depending on the purpose.

### 2.2 Pie Chart

A **pie chart** divides a circle into slices representing each category's share of the total. Each slice's angle is proportional to its category's relative frequency.

**Use it to:** Show how categories make up a whole, especially when there are only a few categories.

\[
\text{Slice angle}=\text{Relative frequency}\times360^\circ
\]

If Tea accounts for 40%:

\[
0.40\times360^\circ=144^\circ
\]

The Tea slice therefore covers \(144^\circ\) of the circle.

A pie chart is most useful when categories form parts of one complete total and the slices can be distinguished easily.

### 2.3 Pareto Chart

A **Pareto chart** is a bar chart in which categories are arranged from highest frequency to lowest frequency. This ordering makes the most common categories immediately visible.

**Use it to:** Identify the leading contributors to a total, such as the most frequent causes of product defects or customer complaints.

Unlike an ordinary bar chart, a Pareto chart uses descending frequency order rather than alphabetical or natural order.

### 2.4 Choosing the Right Display

| Display | Best use |
|---|---|
| Frequency table | Showing exact counts and values, especially when there are many categories. |
| Bar chart | Comparing category frequencies or percentages. |
| Pie chart | Showing each category's share of the whole. |
| Pareto chart | Ranking categories from most frequent to least frequent. |

## 3. Best Practices and Misleading Graphs

A good graph should represent the data accurately, make comparisons easy, and avoid visual distortion. A graph can be technically correct in its numbers but still mislead readers through poor design.

### 3.1 Area Principle

The **area principle** states that the visual area used to represent a value should be proportional to the value itself.

For example, if one category has twice the frequency of another, its visual representation should not appear four or nine times as large. The size of a bar, circle, or picture must not exaggerate the underlying difference.

### 3.2 Common Graphing Errors

| Error | Why it is misleading |
|---|---|
| Truncated bar-chart axis | Starting the value axis above zero exaggerates differences between bars. |
| Unequal bar widths | Changes the visual area even when the frequencies do not change. |
| Distorted shapes or 3D effects | Makes visual area or volume inconsistent with the represented values. |
| Missing labels or units | Makes the graph difficult to interpret correctly. |
| Too many pie slices | Makes individual proportions difficult to compare. |
| Inconsistent category order | Makes comparisons harder and can hide the intended pattern. |

### 3.3 Round-Off Errors in Pie Charts

Percentages are sometimes rounded for display. The rounded values may add up to slightly more or less than 100%, even when the exact values total 100%.

**Example:**

| Category | Exact Percentage | Rounded Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 22.3% | 22% |
| B | 35.6% | 36% |
| C | 12.6% | 13% |
| D | 11.0% | 11% |
| E | 18.5% | 18% |
| **Total** | **100.0%** | **100%** |

In other datasets, rounding may produce totals such as 99% or 101%. A small difference caused by rounding is not automatically evidence of an incorrect chart. Use the unrounded values when calculating slice angles.

### Best Practices Checklist

1. Give the graph a clear title and label its categories.
2. State whether values represent counts, proportions, or percentages.
3. Keep bar widths consistent and use a zero baseline for bar charts.
4. Arrange Pareto-chart bars in descending frequency order.
5. Use pie charts only when the categories form a whole and the slices are easy to distinguish.
6. Avoid unnecessary 3D effects and distorted visual areas.
7. Check totals and account for possible rounding.

## 4. Mode and Median

Mode and median describe the most common or central category. Their use depends on whether the categorical variable has a meaningful order.

### 4.1 Mode

The **mode** is the category with the highest frequency. It identifies the most common response in a dataset.

Using the beverage table, **Tea is the mode** because it occurs 8 times, more than any other category.

The mode can be identified as:
- The tallest bar in a bar chart.
- The largest slice in a pie chart.
- The first category in a correctly ordered Pareto chart.

A dataset can have more than one mode when multiple categories tie for the highest frequency.

**Mode is meaningful for both nominal and ordinal categorical data.**

### 4.2 Median

The **median** is the middle observation after arranging data in its natural order.

For categorical data, a meaningful order is essential:

| Data type | Is median meaningful? |
|---|---|
| Nominal | No — categories have no natural order. |
| Ordinal | Yes — categories can be ranked. |

**Example:** A survey records 20 ordered satisfaction responses.

| Satisfaction | Frequency | Cumulative Frequency |
|---|---:|---:|
| Poor | 2 | 2 |
| Average | 4 | 6 |
| Good | 8 | 14 |
| Excellent | 6 | 20 |

For 20 observations, the two middle positions are the 10th and 11th. Both fall in the **Good** category.

**Median = Good**

Cumulative frequency helps locate the middle observation without listing every response individually.

For an even number of observations, locate both middle positions. If they fall in different categories, report the two middle categories; do not average category labels.

**Important:** Alphabetical order does not make nominal categories ordinal. For example, categories named Academy A, B, C, D, and E do not have a meaningful ranking merely because of their labels. Their median is not defined.

### 4.3 Mode vs. Median

| Basis | Mode | Median |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Most frequent category | Middle category |
| Nominal data | Applicable | Not applicable |
| Ordinal data | Applicable | Applicable |
| Determined by | Highest frequency | Ordered position |

**Mean is not meaningful for categorical variables** because category labels do not support meaningful arithmetic operations.

## 5. Quick Revision

### Essential Formulas

\[
\text{Relative frequency}=\frac{f}{n}
\]

\[
\text{Percentage}=\frac{f}{n}\times100
\]

\[
\text{Frequency}=n\times\text{Relative frequency}
\]

\[
\text{Pie-slice angle}=\text{Relative frequency}\times360^\circ
\]

### Key Concepts

| Concept | Remember |
|---|---|
| Frequency | Count in a category |
| Relative frequency | Fraction of the total |
| Cumulative frequency | Running total in category order |
| Bar chart | Compares category frequencies |
| Pie chart | Shows parts of a whole |
| Pareto chart | Ranks categories by descending frequency |
| Area principle | Visual area must be proportional to the represented value |
| Round-off error | Rounded percentages may not total exactly 100% |
| Mode | Most frequent category |
| Median | Middle category; meaningful only for ordered categories |

## 6. Practice Questions

**1.** In a survey of 50 people, 20 select Category A. Find its relative frequency and percentage.

**2.** If a category has relative frequency 0.25 in a dataset of 200 observations, find its frequency.

**3.** Which display is generally better for comparing category frequencies: a bar chart or a pie chart?

**4.** What is the main purpose of a Pareto chart?

**5.** What does the area principle require?

**6.** Why might rounded percentages in a pie chart add up to 99% or 101%?

**7.** Can a nominal variable have a median? Why?

**8.** A satisfaction survey records 10 Poor, 15 Average, 25 Good, and 10 Excellent responses. Identify the mode.

**9.** Can a dataset have more than one mode?

**10.** Why is the mean not meaningful for categorical data?

<details>
<summary><strong>Answer Key</strong></summary>

1. Relative frequency = \(20/50=0.40\); percentage = 40%.
2. Frequency = \(200\times0.25=50\).
3. Bar chart.
4. To rank categories from highest to lowest frequency and highlight the largest contributors.
5. The visual area representing a value should be proportional to that value.
6. Because individual percentages have been rounded.
7. No. Nominal categories have no natural order.
8. Good, with 25 responses.
9. Yes. Multiple categories may tie for the highest frequency.
10. Category labels cannot be meaningfully added or divided to calculate an average.
