## 1. Introduction to Association

**Association** describes how two variables are related. The method used to study the relationship depends on the types of variables.

| Variable types | Main method |
|---|---|
| Two categorical variables | Contingency table and relative frequencies |
| Two numerical variables | Scatterplot, covariance, and Pearson correlation |
| One categorical and one numerical variable | Point-biserial correlation (for a binary categorical variable) |

**Important:** Association indicates a relationship between variables; it does not, by itself, establish causation.

---

## 2. Association Between Two Categorical Variables

A **contingency table** displays the frequency distribution of two categorical variables together. Each cell shows the number of observations belonging to a particular combination of categories.

### 2.1 Contingency Table

**Example:** A survey records whether 100 students attend a workshop and whether they pass an assessment.

| | Passed | Not passed | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Attended | 36 | 14 | 50 |
| Did not attend | 24 | 26 | 50 |
| **Total** | **60** | **40** | **100** |

- **Joint frequency:** The count in an individual cell, such as 36 students who attended and passed.
- **Marginal frequency:** The row or column total, such as 50 students who attended or 60 who passed.
- **Grand total:** The total number of observations, here 100.

### 2.2 Relative Frequencies

Relative frequencies convert counts into proportions. They help compare categories, particularly when group sizes differ.

**Overall relative frequency:**

[
\text{Overall relative frequency}=\frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Grand total}}
]

For students who attended and passed:

[
\frac{36}{100}=0.36=36\%
]

**Row relative frequency:**

[
\text{Row relative frequency}=\frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Row total}}
]

Among students who attended:

[
\frac{36}{50}=0.72=72\%
]

**Column relative frequency:**

[
\text{Column relative frequency}=\frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Column total}}
]

Among students who passed:

[
\frac{36}{60}=0.60=60\%
]

### 2.3 Interpreting Association

Compare the pass proportions within each attendance group:

- Attended: (36/50=72\%\)
- Did not attend: (24/50=48\%\)

The pass proportions differ, suggesting an association between workshop attendance and assessment outcome in this sample.

**Remember:** Compare relative frequencies with the correct row or column denominator. Raw counts alone can be misleading when group sizes are unequal.

## 3. Association Between Two Numerical Variables

A **scatterplot** displays paired numerical observations as points on a graph. Each point represents one observation, with one variable on the horizontal axis and the other on the vertical axis.

### 3.1 Reading a Scatterplot

A scatterplot helps describe an association through three main features:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Direction | Whether the variables tend to increase together or move in opposite directions |
| Form | Whether the pattern is linear or non-linear |
| Strength | How closely the points follow the overall pattern |

**Direction:**

- **Positive association:** Larger values of one variable tend to occur with larger values of the other.
- **Negative association:** Larger values of one variable tend to occur with smaller values of the other.
- **No clear association:** The points show no consistent pattern.

**Strength:** Points close to a clear pattern indicate a stronger association. Points widely scattered around the pattern indicate a weaker association.

### 3.2 Outliers and Interpretation

An **outlier** is a point that lies unusually far from the general pattern. It can strongly affect numerical measures of association, especially covariance and correlation.

A scatterplot should be examined before calculating or interpreting a correlation coefficient. Also, a linear measure may not describe a strong non-linear relationship well.

## 4. Covariance

**Covariance** measures how two numerical variables vary together. Its sign indicates the direction of their linear co-variation.

### 4.1 Formula

For a population:

[
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu_X)(y_i-\mu_Y)}{N}
]

For a sample:

[
s_{XY}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{n-1}
]

Where:

- (x_i,y_i) are paired observations.
- (\mu_X,\mu_Y) are population means.
- (\bar{x},\bar{y}) are sample means.
- (N) is the population size and (n) is the sample size.

### 4.2 Interpreting Covariance

| Covariance | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Positive | The variables tend to move in the same direction. |
| Negative | The variables tend to move in opposite directions. |
| Zero | There is no linear co-variation in the data. |

**Worked example:** Consider three paired observations:

| (X) | (Y) |
|---:|---:|
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 6 |

The sample means are:

[
\bar{x}=2,\qquad \bar{y}=4
]

The sum of the products of deviations is:

[
(1-2)(2-4)+(2-2)(4-4)+(3-2)(6-4)=4
]

Therefore, sample covariance is:

[
s_{XY}=\frac{4}{3-1}=2
]

**Sample covariance = 2**, indicating positive co-variation.

**Limitation:** Covariance depends on the units and scales of the variables. Its magnitude is therefore difficult to compare across different datasets.

## 5. Pearson Correlation Coefficient

The **Pearson correlation coefficient** measures the direction and strength of the linear relationship between two numerical variables. Unlike covariance, it is standardized and unit-free.

### 5.1 Formula

For a population:

[
\rho_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}
]

For a sample:

[
r=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\bar{x})^2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}
]

Where (\sigma_X,\sigma_Y) are population standard deviations and (r) is the sample correlation coefficient.

### 5.2 Range and Interpretation

The Pearson correlation coefficient always lies between -1 and +1.

| Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| (r=+1) | Perfect positive linear relationship |
| (0<r<+1) | Positive linear relationship |
| (r=0) | No linear relationship |
| (-1<r<0) | Negative linear relationship |
| (r=-1) | Perfect negative linear relationship |

The closer the absolute value of (r) is to 1, the stronger the linear relationship.

**Worked example:** For the paired observations in Section 4:

[
X=(1,2,3),\qquad Y=(2,4,6)
]

The values follow the exact relationship (Y=2X). Their points lie on a straight line with positive slope.

[
r=+1
]

**Pearson correlation = +1**, indicating a perfect positive linear relationship.

### 5.3 Important Limitations

- Correlation measures **linear** association; it may not capture a non-linear pattern.
- Outliers can substantially change the value of (r).
- Correlation does not establish causation.
- A correlation of zero means no linear relationship, not necessarily no relationship of any kind.

## 6. Point-Biserial Correlation Coefficient

The **point-biserial correlation coefficient** measures the relationship between one numerical variable and one binary categorical variable (a variable with exactly two categories).

The binary variable is represented using two codes, commonly 0 and 1.

### 6.1 Formula

[
r_{pb}=\frac{M_1-M_0}{s_Y}\sqrt{pq}
]

Where:

- (M_1) is the mean of the numerical variable for the group coded 1.
- (M_0) is the mean for the group coded 0.
- (s_Y) is the population standard deviation of all numerical observations.
- (p) is the proportion of observations coded 1.
- (q) is the proportion coded 0, so (p+q=1).

### 6.2 Worked Example

Consider test scores for two groups:

| Group code | Scores |
|---|---|
| 0 | 2, 4 |
| 1 | 6, 8 |

Group means:

[
M_0=\frac{2+4}{2}=3,\qquad M_1=\frac{6+8}{2}=7
]

There are two observations in each group, so:

[
p=0.5,\qquad q=0.5
]

The overall mean is 5 and the population standard deviation is:

[
s_Y=\sqrt{\frac{(2-5)^2+(4-5)^2+(6-5)^2+(8-5)^2}{4}}
=\sqrt{5}
]

Substituting:

[
r_{pb}=\frac{7-3}{\sqrt{5}}\sqrt{0.5\times0.5}
\approx0.894
]

**Point-biserial correlation ≈ +0.894**, indicating a strong positive relationship for this coding.

**Interpretation:** The group coded 1 has a higher average score than the group coded 0. Reversing the 0 and 1 codes reverses the sign of the coefficient, but not its magnitude.

## 7. Quick Revision

### Essential Formulas

**Relative frequencies**

[
\text{Overall} = \frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Grand total}}
\qquad
\text{Row} = \frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Row total}}
\]

[
\text{Column} = \frac{\text{Cell frequency}}{\text{Column total}}
]

**Covariance (sample)**

[
s_{XY}=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{n-1}
]

**Pearson correlation (sample)**

[
r=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}
{\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2\sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2}}
]

**Point-biserial correlation**

[
r_{pb}=\frac{M_1-M_0}{s_Y}\sqrt{pq}
]

### Key Concepts

| Concept | Remember |
|---|---|
| Contingency table | Joint frequency distribution of two categorical variables |
| Joint frequency | Count in an individual cell |
| Marginal frequency | Row or column total |
| Relative frequency | Count expressed as a proportion |
| Scatterplot | Graph of paired numerical observations |
| Covariance | Direction of co-variation; depends on units |
| Pearson correlation | Direction and strength of linear association; ranges from -1 to +1 |
| Point-biserial correlation | Association between a numerical variable and a binary variable |
| Association | Does not, by itself, prove causation |
